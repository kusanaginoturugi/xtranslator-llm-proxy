Entry, 3rd of Frostfall. The caravan reached the pass two days late. Snow had buried the old road, and we spent most of the morning digging the wagons free while the mules complained. Our guide, a quiet Nord named Halvar, insisted we keep moving before nightfall. He said the wolves in these hills grow bold when the moons are hidden, and he did not like the look of the clouds. I asked him how he could tell, and he only shrugged and pointed at the ravens circling over the ridge. Entry, 4th of Frostfall. We made camp in the ruins of a watchtower. Halvar found fresh tracks near the gate, larger than any wolf I have seen, and he made us keep the fire burning through the night. None of us slept well. In the morning one of the mules was gone, its rope chewed clean through. Halvar said nothing, but he sharpened his axe before breakfast and kept it across his knees while he ate. Entry, 5th of Frostfall. We are half a day from Whiterun now, and the road is clear. The merchants are already arguing about prices again, which I take as a sign that their fear has passed. I am not so sure. Halvar has asked me to write down everything we saw, in case the Jarl's steward wants to hear of it. So here it is: the tracks, the chewed rope, the ravens, and the sound we all heard just before dawn, a long low howl that did not sound like any beast I know.
On the Nature of Soul Gems, a Lecture for Apprentices. Every enchanter must first understand the vessel before attempting the craft. A soul gem is not merely a stone that glows; it is a cage, and like any cage it can be too small, too weak, or too full. The lesser gems, which you will find in any market from Solitude to Riften, can hold only the faint spark of a rabbit or a mudcrab. These are adequate for practice but will never produce an enchantment worth selling. The common and greater gems hold more, and with patience a skilled hunter can fill them with the souls of bears, trolls, or the restless dead. The grand gem is the largest of the white stones, and a filled grand gem is worth more than many apprentices earn in a year. Treat it with care. A cracked gem leaks its soul slowly, and you will not notice until the enchantment you made with it begins to fade. Then there is the black soul gem, which I mention only so that you will recognize it and refuse it. A black gem can hold the soul of a person, and the means of filling it are forbidden in every hold. I have known two enchanters who believed they could use such gems wisely. One is in the dungeon of Castle Dour, and the other is buried outside Morthal under a stone with no name. Learn from them. Now, to the practice itself. To capture a soul, cast the trapping spell on your quarry and make sure the creature dies while the spell still holds. If you are too slow, the soul escapes, and you will have killed for nothing. If the gem is too small, the soul escapes as well, so match the gem to the prey. When you have a filled gem, bring it to the arcane enchanter along with the item you wish to improve. The enchanter will draw the soul out and bind it to the object, and the gem will be consumed. Do not be discouraged if your first attempts are weak. Every enchantment you make teaches your hands and your mind something new, and in time the work becomes easier. Finally, a word of warning about recharging. A weapon enchanted with fire or frost will lose its charge with every blow. Carry spare gems on long journeys, and refill your weapon before you need it, not after. A sword that sputters in the middle of a fight is worse than a plain blade, because you trusted it. If you take nothing else from this lecture, remember three things. First, never fill a gem larger than the soul requires, because the empty space is wasted. Second, never buy a gem that has already been filled unless you trust the seller, because a weak soul in a grand gem looks the same as a strong one until you use it. Third, keep a written record of every enchantment you make, with the gem, the soul, and the result. In ten years that record will be worth more than any book in this college.
